Sec-01The launch

01Foresight, made a machine.

Today we are introducing Foresight Machines. We are building AI systems that model the world around an institution, examine the consequences of its choices, and learn from what happens next.

A bank should be able to prepare for a run, an insurer to underwrite a hard-to-price specialty risk, a developer to weigh a power delay before committing capital. Our ambition is to make that foresight a permanent capability of institutions.

Every institution already has a model of the future. A quant expresses it in equations, an analyst in a forecast, an investment committee in the assumptions behind an approval. Demand will hold. Funding will remain available. A supplier will deliver. These beliefs determine what gets financed, insured, and built, yet the connections between them often go unexamined. The world they describe does not respect the boundaries between departments, disciplines, or markets.

From a super El Niño that could unsettle harvests and food prices, to an AI buildout that strains power grids and construction capacity, to digital dollars that carry banking risk into software, each development reaches beyond the field in which it begins. The surprise often lies in the interaction: a shortage prompts an export restriction, a power delay becomes a financing problem, a frozen deposit becomes a run on a different institution. Understanding the parts is only the beginning of understanding what they can do together.

SourcesEl Niño impactsAI and energy

An institution needs a way to follow those connections and test its choices against them. The model behind a consequential decision should be something it can inspect, run, and improve.

Our team brings together forecasters, builders of agent-based simulations, capital allocators, and a co-developer of synthetic control methods. Each addresses a different part of the problem: what may happen, how it could unfold, and what an intervention would change.

02The challenge: turn foresight into an instrument

Superforecasting showed that judgment about the future can be trained, scored, and improved. AI is now reaching that standard. In the Summer 2026 Metaculus Cup, AI systems finished first and second overall, ahead of every human entrant. This is a significant advance in our ability to anticipate events.

SourceMetaculus results

Prediction markets such as Kalshi aggregate beliefs into prices. Robin Hanson’s futarchy goes further, proposing that decisions be guided by markets estimating outcomes under alternative policies. Both make expectations explicit and give people a reason to improve them.

SourcesKalshiFutarchy

An institution must connect those expectations to its own choices. A forecast of higher interest rates is an input; deciding whether to refinance requires a model of its debt, cash flows, and alternatives. The same forecast can justify different actions for different institutions. What matters is how an event reaches the institution, what it can do in response, and how that response changes the outcome.

Weather forecasting offers a precedent. Observations feed models of the atmosphere; forecasts are checked against the weather that arrives; errors inform the next improvement. This discipline creates value wherever a better view of tomorrow changes what people do today: routing aircraft, dispatching power, planning harvests, and moving goods.

SourcesHow forecasts are madeForecasts in use

Markets and institutions introduce a further difficulty: people respond. A depositor withdraws because others might, a competitor invests because demand is rising, a regulator intervenes because a market is failing. A decision changes the conditions under which other decisions are made.

We want to bring the discipline of weather forecasting to this world of interacting decisions. Two capabilities are opening the way: the ability to maintain richer models at lower cost, and to train and test their judgment against outcomes.

03Unlock 1: Models that stay current

Much of the knowledge needed to model an institution sits in contracts, filings, operating reports, and the heads of specialists. Turning it into a working model has required painstaking translation. Keeping it current has required doing that work again.

Language models can take on more of this translation: extracting an obligation from a contract, a dependency from an operating report, a constraint from a regulation. Connected to current observations, these capabilities make it more practical to maintain a model as the world changes.

The model must preserve the nature of what it contains. A contractual payment is an obligation; a power limit is a physical constraint; a forecast of withdrawals is an estimate of behavior. Each needs an appropriate method and a traceable basis. Fluent descriptions alone do not establish how a system works.

With those relationships represented, an institution can compare actions against the same evidence. It can examine what drives the result, where uncertainty changes the choice, and which missing fact would be worth learning before it commits.

04Running a decision

In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank failed with $3.3 billion of Circle’s USDC reserves on deposit. USDC broke its dollar peg. Authorities subsequently protected all SVB deposits, restoring access to the funds. A digital dollar transferable around the clock had depended on bank deposits its issuer could not immediately reach.

SourcesCircle’s accountOfficial intervention

Consider the decision before such a crisis: how should an issuer arrange its reserves so it can keep meeting redemptions under stress?

A foresight machine would connect the issuer’s holdings to its banking partners, settlement arrangements, and redemption obligations. It would examine how rising rates and tighter funding could affect several counterparties at once, and how doubts about reserve access could accelerate demand for redemption. Spreading cash across banks may still leave it exposed to a common shock.

The issuer could then compare changes to its banking mix, reserve liquidity, and backup funding. For each combination, the machine would estimate how much cash could be reached by a given deadline, how much redemption demand it could meet, and what that protection would cost. Policy support would be a scenario to test alongside delayed intervention and no intervention.

As evidence changed, the model would show which assumptions had weakened and which choices remained viable. The decision would turn on the time an issuer could withstand interrupted access, the price of extending that time, and the conditions under which its defenses would fail.

The output is a rehearsal of the decision, while there is still time to make it differently.

05Unlock 2: Learning from outcomes

A model earns trust by exposing itself to correction. Its expectations must be recorded before events unfold, its errors preserved after they do, its revisions tested before they are relied upon.

In the reserve example, the machine would commit its estimates of withdrawals, cash availability, and funding costs. Outcomes would test those estimates. Repeatedly understating the speed of redemptions would reveal a weakness; a proposed correction would have to improve performance on cases withheld from its development.

This is what we mean by self-correcting: a process that makes mistakes visible and tests whether a change remedies them.

Historical events can accelerate that process, provided later knowledge is excluded from the evidence and the model being tested. Research on training language models with resolved forecasting questions offers evidence that outcomes can improve predictive accuracy and calibration. Live forecasts, recorded in advance, remain the continuing test.

SourceFuture-as-Label research

Judging a decision requires another distinction. We observe what followed the action taken; the effects of alternatives must be estimated. A fortunate outcome can reward a poor decision, and an unlucky one can punish a sound decision. The machine therefore needs a record across many cases, with forecast accuracy assessed separately from evidence about what its actions changed.

Over time, an institution gains a history it can learn from: what it believed, why it acted, and where its understanding failed.

06Foresight as an institutional capability

An institution should be able to carry what it learns from one decision into the next. The evidence behind an investment, the dependencies behind a supply commitment, and the assumptions behind an underwriting decision should remain available for examination. When conditions change, the institution should know which commitments need another look.

Better foresight can also expand what an institution is willing to undertake. An insurer can examine whether a hard-to-price specialty risk becomes insurable under different terms. A business can test whether a new market becomes viable with a different partner, a smaller initial commitment, or a later launch. The machine should help identify the conditions that make an opportunity worth pursuing.

As the machines earn trust, we intend for them to assume responsibility within defined mandates. An allocation can be made, monitored, and revised by the same system, with the evidence for each action available for inspection.

The Foresight Machines mission

Sec-01 06

We want every consequential institution to be able to run the world forward before it acts. We are building self-correcting machines that can earn the trust to go further: to allocate capital, underwrite risk, hedge exposure, and secure supply on the strength of their own record. Their models should remain open to challenge, their mistakes visible, and their judgment capable of improving with experience.

Foresight should be a permanent capability of every consequential institution.

Weather forecasting earned its place in the economy through use, measurement, and improvement. We want foresight about the systems people build to earn the same standing.

The hardest decisions are not unknowable. They are unrun.
