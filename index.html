Sec-01The launch · September 2026

01Foresight, made a machine.

At Foresight Machines we build AI systems that hold a living model of the world around an institution — its markets, counterparties, rules, and exposures — and run that world forward before the institution acts: in finance, insurance, energy, supply chains, and statecraft.

The output is not a prediction. It is a rehearsal: how the consequences of a decision unfold, which assumptions carry the plan, and which course of action survives the futures no one can rule out.

Today, Foresight Machines is coming out of stealth. Our team brings together people who forecast the world, people who model it, and people who have acted on both: superforecasters, AI scientists, capital allocators, pioneers of agent-based simulation for financial institutions, and a co-inventor of synthetic control methods, which economists use to estimate what would have happened without a policy. Predicting a world is one discipline; predicting a world you are about to change takes all of them.

02The challenge: turn foresight into an instrument

Foreseeing is as old as planting a seed. Acting today against a world that exists only tomorrow is the move underneath everything an institution does: a loan is underwritten into a future economy, a risk is priced into a future climate, capital is committed to a market that will not hold still while it arrives.

That move keeps getting harder. From a super El Niño bearing down on harvests, shipping, and power grids, to AI data centers bidding for the same power, to programmable capital scaling toward trillions, the world an institution decides into has never been more tightly coupled. Rules fragment across jurisdictions, and a change in one system reaches another before the next planning cycle begins.

Yet the model of the future that every one of these decisions rests on still lives where it always has: three tabs of a spreadsheet, a paragraph of an investment memo, an analyst's conviction, a conversation before the board meets. It is scattered, implicit, revised unevenly, and rarely checked against what happened. Institutions measure their people, their processes, and their quarters. Almost none measure their model of the future.

Consider a decision, not a question:

We can make this move now, in a year, or never. What does each cost in the futures we can’t rule out?

The last twenty years produced real progress on questions about the future. Philip Tetlock showed that judgment can be trained and scored: his superforecasters, generalists graded with proper scoring rules, outperformed intelligence analysts with classified access in tournaments run by the US intelligence community. Machines have since caught up: in the Summer 2026 Metaculus Cup, AI systems finished first and second, ahead of every human competitor, and calibrated probabilities on world events are becoming abundant and cheap. Prediction markets like Polymarket and Kalshi pay for information and penalize miscalibration, and Robin Hanson's futarchy and Vitalik Buterin's info finance propose letting markets steer decisions, governance included.

None of this decides anything yet. Superforecasts do not run the C-suite. Markets cover what people will bet on, not what institutions must resolve. Machine forecasters answer questions one at a time, each answer separate from the last. The gap is not accuracy. A probability is a fact about the world; a decision is an act upon it. A number tells you the weather. It does not tell you whether to sail, which route holds if the wind turns, or when to put back into port.

Two capabilities now close that gap.

03Unlock 1: Faster feedback

Every claim with a deadline eventually resolves, and the resolution is a label no one has to write and no one can fake. A model can be trained on masked history — it is March 2025, and it knows only what was knowable then — and scored when events resolve. A human forecaster cannot unlearn a year and predict it again; a machine can, thousands of times. Judgment has never had feedback this fast, and it works: a 32-billion-parameter model trained this way outpredicts one seven times its size.

We extend the same discipline from questions to decisions. Before an outcome is known, a foresight machine commits to what it expects from each action under consideration: what breaks the plan, what carries it, what each alternative yields. When reality arrives, the machine is scored and the score is kept. Each resolved decision improves the next model of the world, and gives the institution a measured record of its own judgment.

04Unlock 2: World models, cheap and current

Synthetic population modeling has shown that simulating people at scale can predict what they do better than asking them. But modeling the world around a single decision is still manual work: it takes specialists months, and the model is stale before it is finished. That cost is why institutions model the future so rarely, and why the model is never maintained between decisions.

Language models remove the bottleneck. They can read where an institution's model of the future actually lives — the memos, filings, contracts, and submissions no database ever captured — and assemble it into an explicit state of the world, then keep that state current as evidence arrives. Once the state is explicit, a counterfactual costs compute instead of a planning cycle. If the licensing regime slips six months, what happens to the capital plan? If it slips and rates hold — then what? The machine runs a thousand of these on every decision, and keeps running them as conditions change. And because one model underlies every decision, the claims stay consistent with each other. Forecasters whose beliefs are coherent across related questions are measurably more accurate on each one.

05Machine foresight

We are building machines that maintain the living state of a business, an institution, a sovereign — its assets, obligations, counterparties, rules, and dependencies — update that state as the world changes, run the counterfactuals of what it might do next, and return the actions that stay sound across futures. Today the machine rehearses and the institution decides. Soon no bank will lend, no insurer will price a risk, and no government will set a policy without running the world forward first. Their shareholders, reinsurers, and voters will ask to see the run. The goal is machines trusted to go further: to allocate capital, underwrite risk, hedge exposure, and procure supply on their own record — foresight as infrastructure, a permanent capability of every consequential institution.

Weather forecasting delivers billions of dollars of value a year, and the value arrives where forecasts meet decisions: planting schedules, flight paths, shipping lanes, grid dispatch. We are building that junction for everything an institution decides.

The hardest decisions are not unknowable. They are unrun.
