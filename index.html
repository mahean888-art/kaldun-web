Launching Foresight Machines

01

At Foresight Machines we build AI systems that hold a living model of the world around an institution — its markets, counterparties, rules, and exposures — and run that world forward before the institution acts: in finance, insurance, energy, supply chains, and statecraft. The output is not a prediction. It is a rehearsal: how the consequences of a decision unfold, which assumptions carry the plan, and which course of action survives the futures no one can rule out.

Today, Foresight Machines is coming out of stealth. Our team brings together superforecasters, AI scientists, capital allocators, pioneers of agent-based simulation for financial institutions, and a co-inventor of synthetic control methods, the tool economists reach for to measure what a policy actually did. Predicting a world is one discipline; predicting a world you are about to act on takes all of them.

Synthetic control: a method that builds a weighted comparison from units a policy did not touch, to estimate what would have happened without it.
02

The challenge: turn foresight into an instrument

Foreseeing is as old as planting a seed. Acting today against a world that exists only tomorrow is the move underneath everything an institution does: a loan is underwritten into a future economy, a risk is priced into a future climate, capital is committed to a market that will not hold still while it arrives.

That move keeps getting harder. Capital, supply, energy, and policy now run through tightly coupled networks, and rules fragment across jurisdictions — so a change in one system reaches another before the next planning cycle begins. Meanwhile the institution's model of the future, the thing every one of these decisions rests on, lives where it has always lived: three tabs of a spreadsheet, a paragraph of an investment memo, an analyst's conviction, a conversation before the board meets. It is scattered, implicit, revised unevenly, and rarely checked against what happened. Institutions measure their people, their processes, and their quarters. Almost none measure their model of the future.

03

Consider a decision, not a question:

The last twenty years produced real progress on questions about the future. Philip Tetlock showed that judgment can be trained and scored: his superforecasters, generalists graded with proper scoring rules, matched or beat intelligence analysts with classified access in tournaments run by the US intelligence community. Machines then caught up: in the Summer 2026 Metaculus Cup, AI systems outforecast every human competitor for the first time, and calibrated probabilities on world events are becoming abundant and cheap. Prediction markets like Polymarket and Kalshi pay for information and penalize miscalibration — the working version of Robin Hanson's futarchy and Vitalik Buterin's info finance, where forecasts are meant to steer governance itself.

Proper scoring rule: a score that is best, on average, when the stated probability is the forecaster's honest belief.

None of this decides anything yet. Superforecasts do not run the C-suite. Markets cover what people will bet on, not what institutions must resolve. Machine forecasters answer questions one at a time, each answer separate from the last. The gap is not accuracy. A probability is a fact about the world; a decision is an act upon it. A number tells you the weather. It does not tell you whether to sail, which route holds if the wind turns, or when to put back into port.

Two capabilities now close that gap.

04

Unlock 1: Faster feedback

Every claim about the world eventually resolves, and the resolution is a label no one has to write and no one can fake. A model can be trained on masked history — it is March 2025, and it knows only what was knowable then — and scored when events resolve. A human forecaster cannot unlearn a year and predict it again; a machine can, thousands of times. Judgment has never had feedback this fast, and it works: a 32-billion-parameter model trained this way outpredicts one seven times its size.

Masked history: training on the record up to a date, with everything after it withheld, so the model can be scored on what came next.

We extend the same discipline from questions to decisions. Before an outcome is known, a foresight machine commits to what it expects from each action under consideration: what breaks the plan, what carries it, what each alternative yields. When reality arrives, the machine is scored and the score is kept. Each resolved decision improves the next model of the world, and gives the institution a measured record of its own judgment.

05

Unlock 2: World models, cheap and current

Modeling the world around a decision used to be manual work — synthetic population modeling proved it works at scale, but each model takes specialists months to build and is stale before it is finished. That cost is why institutions model the future so rarely, and why the model is never maintained between decisions.

Language models remove the bottleneck. They can read where an institution's model of the future actually lives — the memos, filings, contracts, and submissions no database ever captured — and assemble it into an explicit state of the world, then keep that state current as evidence arrives. Once the state is explicit, a counterfactual costs compute instead of a planning cycle. If the licensing regime slips six months, what happens to the capital plan? If it slips and rates hold — then what? The machine runs a thousand of these on every decision, and keeps running them as conditions change. And because one model underlies every decision, the claims stay consistent with each other — which is not just tidiness: forecasters whose beliefs stay coherent across related questions are measurably more accurate on each one.

Counterfactual: the same world, run again with one condition changed.
06

Machine foresight

We are building machines that maintain the living state of a business, an institution, a sovereign — its assets, obligations, counterparties, rules, and dependencies — update that state as the world changes, run the counterfactuals of what it might do next, and return the actions that stay sound across futures. Today the machine rehearses and the institution decides. The goal is machines trusted to go further: to allocate capital, underwrite risk, hedge exposure, and procure supply on their own record — foresight as infrastructure, a permanent capability of every consequential institution.

Weather forecasting delivers billions of dollars of value a year, and the value arrives where forecasts meet decisions: planting schedules, flight paths, shipping lanes, grid dispatch. We are building that junction for everything an institution decides.

The hardest decisions are not unknowable. They are unrun.
